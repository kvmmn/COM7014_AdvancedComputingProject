When should a time-series foundation model be trusted?

The problem, what the literature already solves, how the remaining gap will be tested, and the decisions needed to close M1.

01Answer

Routing already exists. A router whose confidence can be checked does not.

Published systems already choose, series by series, between foundation models and classical forecasters. None shows that its confidence can be relied on. This project builds and tests one that does.

When the router says “trust the foundation model, 80% confident”, it should be right about 80% of the time. When it is not confident enough, it falls back to a cheaper classical model, including on a field it never saw in training.

Terms used on this page
Foundation model (FM)A pretrained time-series model used without further training (zero-shot), such as Chronos-2.
Classical fallbackSeasonal Naive, ETS, ARIMA and Theta, plus Croston, SBA and TSB on demand series.
RouterThe small model that reads series characteristics and outputs the trust probability.
CalibratedDecisions made at 80% confidence are right about 80% of the time.
FieldAn application area such as retail, energy or banking. A held-out field is never seen in router training.
OverlapA test series that was in an FM's pretraining data, which would inflate its zero-shot score.
02Evidence and gap

Foundation models win on average and lose on particular series.

Benchmark averages favour foundation models, sparse demand produces clear failures, and existing routers already exploit that. What is missing is evidence that a router's confidence can be relied on.

What exists, and what does not

What “checked confidence” means

Calibration

Stated confidence should match how often the choice is right.

Deferral

Trusting the FM on fewer series should mean fewer errors.

Transfer

The same check must hold on a field the router never saw.

Schematics explain the tests. They are not results.

Defensible to investigate
  • A calibrated probability that the routing choice is correct
  • Explicit trust/defer operating points
  • FM versus classical fallback, including on sparse demand
  • Transfer to a held-out dataset and a held-out field
  • Accuracy, regret and compute trade-offs
Not claimed
  • Per-series routing is new
  • Cross-family model selection is new
  • M5 or Favorita is a novel testbed
  • A wrapper licence covers all source data
  • A benchmark-level leakage flag proves a model is clean on other datasets
03Questions

One main question, four ways it can fail.

Main research question

Can a per-series router give a calibrated probability that trusting a time-series foundation model is the right choice, and does that probability still hold on datasets and fields it was not trained on?

RQ1
Signals · Which series characteristics predict that the FM will win?

Fails if feature-based prediction does no better than always predicting the majority outcome on held-out datasets.

RQ2
Value · Can routing match or beat the best single model at lower cost?

Fails if always-FM or always-classical matches or beats the router on error at equal or lower measured cost.

RQ3 ★
Trust · Is the trust probability calibrated, including on a held-out field?

Fails if held-out calibration error exceeds a pre-declared bound, or error does not fall as coverage is reduced.

RQ4
Sparse demand · Can the router spot intermittent series in advance and send them to the fallback?

Fails if it does no better than the fixed Syntetos–Boylan rule, on demand series only.

★ Central question. Each question can stand as a result even if a later one is weaker.

04Datasets

Every dataset has one job.

The questions need breadth for comparability, sparse demand with published routing results for direct comparison, and one field the router never trains on.

05Method

A small router, a mixed model pool, and a test it cannot memorise.

The router sees series characteristics, never future outcomes. It has to generalise across datasets, report its uncertainty honestly and justify the compute it spends.

How the router is validated

Four scorecards

AccuracyPer-series MASE; CRPS or quantile loss where models give distributions
RegretGap from an oracle that always picks the per-series winner
TrustECE, Brier score, reliability and risk–coverage curves
CostWall-clock inference on declared hardware

Rules that protect the result

  • Fix “winner” in advance. Primary metric and tie margin are declared before evaluation; near-ties go to the cheaper model.
  • Split by dataset, not by row. Leave-one-dataset-out for the router; rolling-origin for the forecasts.
  • Check overlap per checkpoint and dataset. Uncertain pairs are left out of headline results and reported separately.
  • Use intermittency classes only on demand. Not on energy, temperature or prices. sMAPE only on strictly positive series.
  • Size the run first. A smoke test on the project laptop sets a stratified subsample if the full corpus does not fit.
  • Report failure as a result. If confidence cannot be calibrated or does not transfer, that is a valid finding.

07Decisions

Six decisions to close M1.

  1. ContributionCalibrated trust-or-defer, tested for transfer, as the distinction from FAME, Zhang, CARFS, TimeRouter and TW3Cast.
  2. CorpusGIFT-Eval for breadth; M5, Favorita, Car Parts and NN5 for comparison; Ausgrid as the held-out field.
  3. Common groundA univariate core comparison with scaled errors and each dataset's published horizon; covariates outside the core.
  4. ValidationLeave-one-dataset-out testing, energy-free router training for the Ausgrid test, and uncertain overlap pairs excluded from headline results.
  5. ScopeLightGBM as a required reference baseline outside the routing pool; further neural or hybrid models optional.
  6. RightsModel licences, Kaggle terms and the verified upstream conditions of each added dataset.
