When to Trust a Time-Series Foundation Model

For any single time series: should we use a big pre-trained model, or a simple classical one? And how sure can we be?

Kaveh Momeni · Working briefing · Updated 8 October 2026 · No data downloaded or models run

In 30 seconds

  • Problem: benchmarks report averages, but in practice you pick a model for each series separately.
  • What changed: the new article search found that per-series routing across classical, ML and deep models already exists on M5 and Favorita.
  • Gap: the defensible contribution is now narrower: a calibrated probability that trusting a foundation model is the right decision, with an explicit classical fallback and held-out-field testing.
  • So far: 42 logged searches, 15 article-to-dataset extractions, 8 fields covered, and a model–dataset leakage problem identified.
The idea

One decision, per series

A time seriese.g. daily sales of one product
→
Quick profileabout 30 simple statistics
→
Confidence score“how likely is the big model to win here?”
High score → trust it
Use the foundation model.
Low score → fall back
Use a cheap classical or sparse-demand method.

Glossary: foundation model (FM) = large model pre-trained on millions of series, used without retraining. Classical = simple statistical methods such as ETS, ARIMA, Croston.

01 · Why

Averages hide failures

Good news

FMs are among the strongest forecasters on the main benchmark, GIFT-Eval.

Bad news

On sparse retail demand (M5) an FM scored 0.97 vs 0.52 for a tuned LightGBM (error, lower is better).

Missing

No one tells you which series will fail, or how far to trust a model's choice.

Source for the first two: Rubczynski (2026), a review of 543 model comparisons in retail. It finds a real overall FM advantage, so the picture is mixed, not one-sided.

02 · What exists

Routing is established. Reliable trust decisions are not.

* FAME, Zhang's sparse-demand router, CARFS, AutoForecast, FFORMPP, TimeRouter and TW3Cast. Their selection units, expert pools and evaluation targets differ.

The project will not claim that per-series routing is new. The contribution under investigation is a verified trust/defer probability, cross-family fallback, and generalisation beyond the retail datasets used by the closest studies.

03 · Questions

Four questions

RQ1 · Signals
Which series properties predict that an FM will win?
Fails if the properties predict no better than guessing.
RQ2 · Value
Does routing match the best single model at lower cost?
Fails if “always use the FM” wins on accuracy and cost.
RQ3 · Trust
When the router says 80% sure, is it right about 80% of the time?
Measured with reliability diagrams and calibration error.
RQ4 · Sparse demand
Can it spot sparse series by itself and route them well?
Must beat a simple hand-written rule to count.

A negative answer is still a result: finding the minority of series where FMs fail is useful on its own.

The datasets are evidence — and a warning

The same retail datasets recur across the closest model-selection studies. That makes them excellent for comparison, but weak as evidence of originality.

Novelty boundary: “route each series to a model” is already done. The proposed work must show calibrated trust, a clear defer/fallback policy, and generalisation to a dataset family or field not used to train the router.

Sorting sparse demand. Each demand series lands in one of four boxes, based on how often sales happen and how much their size varies:

Erraticfrequent · size jumps
Lumpyrare · size jumps
Smoothfrequent · steady size
Intermittentrare · steady size

Syntetos–Boylan classification. Only for demand data, not for energy or temperature. Expectation to test: FMs do best on “smooth”, worst on “intermittent” and “lumpy”.

The big pitfall: test data the model has already seen. Some models were trained on the very datasets used to test them, so their scores look better than they are. Older Chronos, TimesFM 1–2 and TinyTimeMixer are flagged. Chronos-2, TimesFM-2.5 and MOIRAI are clean on GIFT-Eval. For datasets outside GIFT-Eval this still has to be checked.

If a check fails
  • Test only on time periods after the model's release.
  • Prefer datasets published after the model was trained.
  • Report results with and without the doubtful datasets.
07 · The models

Who is in the pool

Foundation Chronos-2 · TimesFM-2.5 · MOIRAI

Classical Seasonal Naive · ETS · ARIMA · Theta

Sparse demand Croston · SBA · TSB

Strong baseline LightGBM (best known result on M5)

A foundation model gets in only if it is:

  • clean (has not seen the test data)
  • openly licensed for academic use
  • able to run on a 16 GB laptop
  • different in design from the others
Left out, and why
Chronos (original), TimesFM 1–2, TinyTimeMixersaw test data
TimesFM 3.0non-commercial licence

TiRex, Toto, Sundial, Moirai-2not yet checked; possible later

What about LSTM, CNN-LSTM or hybrid ARIMA-LSTM? They are neither zero-shot nor cheap, so they don't fit the router's choice between “big pre-trained” and “simple fallback”. They can still be reported alongside it: one trained neural model (e.g. N-HiTS) and a hybrid ARIMA-LSTM on a subset. CNN-LSTM is left out.

08 · The router

Three rules that keep it honest

Define “win” in advance. One error measure, fixed before running. Near-ties go to the cheaper model.
No hand-written shortcuts. Sparsity is an input, not a hard rule. The hand-written rule becomes a baseline it must beat.
Test on unseen datasets. Train on some datasets, test on others, so it can't memorise.

Why rule 1 matters: on sparse data, some error measures reward predicting zero, others reward predicting the average. The choice can flip the winner.

09 · How we judge it

Four scorecards

Accuracy

Forecast error per series (MASE, quantile loss).

Regret

How much worse than a perfect chooser?

Trust

Does 80% confidence mean right 80% of the time?

Cost

Seconds per forecast on the project laptop.

Plus one curve: error vs share of series sent to the FM, as the confidence threshold moves. That curve is the answer to “when to trust”.

10 · Risks and plan

What could go wrong

  • Leaked test data → per-dataset checks and later time periods.
  • Noisy winners → tie margin; check stability across time windows.
  • Laptop too slow → timing test first, then a balanced subsample.
