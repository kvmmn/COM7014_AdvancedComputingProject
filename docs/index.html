1At a glance

Forecasting benchmarks report how good a foundation model is on average over a dataset. A practitioner needs something else: for this series, should I use the foundation model or a cheaper classical method, and how sure can I be? This project builds and evaluates a small per-series router that answers that question and attaches a confidence score that can be checked.

The working principle is problem-first and data-first: the corpus and the model pool are chosen because they let the research questions be answered, not the other way round.

The evidence is mixed rather than one-sided, and that is precisely why a per-series decision is worth studying. The table separates what each source shows from what it does not show.

The cost motive, stated carefully. An FM forward pass is far more expensive than fitting ETS or Croston, but the review reports that consumer-hardware FM inference remains viable up to roughly 30K series with daily re-forecasting. Cost is therefore treated as a measured quantity in this project, not as an assumption.

3What already exists, and the gap

Choosing a forecasting model from series features is an established line of work. FFORMS (Talagala et al.) and FFORMA (Montero-Manso et al., 2020) selected or weighted classical models from time-series features. TimeRouter and TW3Cast route among foundation models. More recent retail studies — Zhang et al., CARFS and especially FAME — already cross model-family boundaries. The defensible gap is therefore not “foundation versus classical routing” by itself; it is whether that decision can be made with a probability that is calibrated, auditable and useful for deferral.

Honest positioning. Routing and cross-family model choice are not new. The narrower contribution is a calibrated estimate of whether the selected model is likely to be correct, an explicit defer policy when confidence is inadequate, and leakage-aware validation on datasets and fields not used to train the router.

Query families. Each query combined a task term (forecasting, demand forecasting, load forecasting), a data term (benchmark, dataset, corpus) and a field term (retail, energy, transport, finance, weather, healthcare, industrial, cloud telemetry). Routing literature was searched through Google Scholar and publisher indexes with model selection, algorithm selection, meta-learning forecasting, FFORMS and the exact phrase "forecast model selection" time series dataset.

Stopping rule. The initial rule — three consecutive searches without a strong new candidate, at least three fields covered, and one confirmed intermittency-heavy collection — was met. R7 deliberately reopened the search because registry evidence alone was too shallow. It closed after the core routing line had been traced to concrete evaluation datasets and the additional searches produced repetition rather than a new high-priority corpus.

5.2 What the article extraction changed

The paper-level pass changed both the novelty claim and the corpus design. Cross-family routing is already demonstrated on retail data, so the project must earn its contribution through calibrated confidence, deferral and stronger out-of-dataset validation. It also revealed which datasets have a clear experimental role rather than merely being available.

5.3 Remaining limitations

  • Scopus Preview exposed bibliographic records but full institutional Scopus search was not available in this pass.
  • The 15 records are a purposive extraction around routing and dataset choice, not a claim of an exhaustive systematic review.
  • Dataset licences, stable download routes and foundation-model training overlap remain hard gates before any download.
  • No dataset has been downloaded and no model has been run; every proposed size, zero rate and split is still source-reported.

6Dataset analysis

6.1 Units of analysis

Benchmarks are nested. Counting at the wrong level is a common source of confusion (GIFT-Eval is described as 23 datasets, its repository lists 28 dataset entries, and the leaderboard scores 97 configurations).

Benchmark→Dataset→Frequency variant→Configuration (dataset × frequency × horizon)→Series→Forecast window

The router decides at the series level; accuracy is measured per window; generalisation is tested across datasets.

6.2 Structural attributes recorded for every dataset

Shape

Number of series, length distribution, frequency, forecast horizon, missing-value pattern.

Signal

Seasonal strength, trend, non-stationarity, spectral entropy, share of zeros, inter-demand interval p, CV² of non-zero values.

Context

Univariate or multivariate; dynamic covariates (prices, promotions, weather); static metadata (store, category); hierarchy.

Governance

Licence, provenance and version, file format, and whether each candidate model saw it during pre-training.

The covariate attribute matters because model families differ in what they can use: univariate FMs ignore promotions and prices, while covariate-aware models (LightGBM, and Chronos-2 by design) can use them.

6.3 Intermittency classification (Syntetos–Boylan)

For non-negative demand series only, each series is placed by its average inter-demand interval p and the squared coefficient of variation of non-zero demand CV². The classification does not apply to energy load, temperature or prices.

Erratic

p ≤ 1.32 · CV² > 0.49

Regular occurrence, highly variable size.

Lumpy

p > 1.32 · CV² > 0.49

Rare occurrence, highly variable size. Hardest class.

Smooth

p ≤ 1.32 · CV² ≤ 0.49

Regular occurrence, stable size.

Intermittent

p > 1.32 · CV² ≤ 0.49

Rare occurrence, stable size. Croston-type territory.

horizontal: occurrence becomes rarer → · vertical: size becomes more variable ↑

Hypothesis, not finding: FMs are expected to do best on smooth series and worst on intermittent and lumpy ones. This mapping is what RQ4 tests; the SBC rule itself is used as a baseline router.

7Model pool and selection logic

7.1 Criteria for a foundation model to enter the pool

  1. No leakage flag on GIFT-Eval, and a per-dataset check for the added sets.
  2. Open weights with a licence that permits academic use.
  3. Runs on the project machine (16 GB Apple M3) within the time budget — confirmed by a smoke test.
  4. Architectural diversity, so pool-size ablations mean something.
  5. Maintained code and a documented inference interface.

7.4 Where do LSTM, CNN-LSTM and hybrid ARIMA–LSTM fit?

They are not "classical" in the statistical sense. It helps to separate three paradigms by how a model is trained, not by architecture:

Local statistical

ETS, ARIMA, Croston: a few parameters fitted per series in milliseconds. Transparent, no training pipeline.

Trained on the target data

Local (one network per series, e.g. hybrid ARIMA–LSTM as in Zhang, 2003) or global (one network across all series: DeepAR, N-HiTS, global LightGBM). Needs a training pipeline and hyper-parameter tuning.

Zero-shot foundation

Chronos-2, TimesFM, MOIRAI: pre-trained elsewhere; no training on the target data; one forward pass per forecast.

Decision rule: a model enters only if it helps answer an RQ. The router's question is “trust the zero-shot FM, or fall back to something cheap?”. A trained deep model is neither, and adding it to the routing pool increases label noise and compute without serving RQ1–RQ4. Proposal: (1) global LightGBM as the required trained baseline; (2) one global neural model (N-HiTS or DeepAR) as an optional comparator; (3) hybrid ARIMA–LSTM as an optional practitioner baseline on a stratified subset — reported alongside the router, not inside it. CNN-LSTM adds little beyond these and is left out.

8Router design

1 · SeriesHistory up to the forecast origin only.
2 · Featurescatch22 + share of zeros, p, CV², length, frequency, covariate flag.
3 · One-vs-all scorerOne classifier per candidate: “will this model be within the margin of the best?”
4 · Calibrated scoreProbability that trusting the FM is the right choice.
5 · DecisionTrust the FM if score ≥ τ; otherwise fall back.
Trust FM — the best-scoring foundation model forecasts.
Fall back — the best-scoring classical, intermittent or tree model forecasts.
τ is a dial — sweeping it traces the risk–coverage curve (§9).

8.1 Design decisions that determine the results

DecisionChoiceWhy
Routing labelA model “wins” a series if its error is within a pre-registered margin (e.g. 2–5%) of the best; ties go to the cheaper model.Without a margin, tiny noisy differences become labels, and cost is ignored.
Error metric behind the labelFixed in advance: MASE for point forecasts, weighted quantile loss for probabilistic forecasts; label sensitivity reported for both.The metric decides the winner. On intermittent data an absolute-error metric rewards forecasting zero (the median); a squared-error metric rewards the mean, which Croston targets.
Intermittency handlingSBC statistics are router inputs; no hard-coded sparsity gate.A fixed gate would make RQ4 untestable. The SBC rule is instead a baseline the router must beat.
CalibrationOne-vs-all structure (Verma & Nalisnick, 2022); post-hoc recalibration (Platt / isotonic) as an ablation.The one-vs-all structure is designed to yield calibrated probabilities of expert correctness.
Meta-learnerGradient-boosted trees or logistic regression on the feature table.Fast, small data needs, inspectable.

8.2 Baselines the router must beat

  • Always use the best single FM · always use the best classical model.
  • Syntetos–Boylan rule (intermittent/lumpy → Croston/SBA/TSB, otherwise FM).
  • Naive rule by series length · FFORMS-style feature selector without FMs.
  • Oracle selector (upper bound, used to compute regret).

10Threats to validity

ThreatRiskMitigation
Pre-training leakageInflated FM scores → router learns to over-trust FMs.Per-dataset leakage matrix (§6.5); temporal hold-out; with/without sensitivity.
Label noiseNear-ties produce unstable labels.Margin and cost-aware tie rule; report label stability across windows.
Metric dependenceWinner changes with the metric.Metric fixed in advance; sensitivity analysis.
Dataset dominanceLarge datasets dominate the router's training.Per-dataset weighting; leave-one-dataset-out.
Hardware-specific costTimings do not transfer to other machines.Report hardware, batch size and settings; compare relative, not absolute, costs.
Hyper-parameter asymmetryTuned baselines vs untuned FMs (or the reverse).Default settings for all, documented; any tuning applied equally.

11Feasibility and scope

  • Smoke test first: time each FM per 1,000 series on the 16 GB M3 (CPU and Apple-GPU backends). The full checkpoint list is frozen only after this.
  • Stratified subsample of GIFT-Eval configurations if the full 97 do not fit the budget, stratified by domain, frequency and SBC class.
  • Staged results that stand alone: Stage 1 (per-series model comparison and feature table) is a result even if the router is weak; Stage 2 (router) is a result even if calibration is poor; Stage 3 (calibration and cost) completes the contribution.
  • Ethics: public secondary data, no participants; still subject to the module's proposal and ethics approval before any download.

12Title options

A good title should name the decision (trust or fall back), the unit (per series), the distinctive element (calibration) and the two families. Changing the title may require updating the ethics record, which is a point for discussion.

Preferred
When to Trust a Time-Series Foundation Model: Calibrated Per-Series Routing Between Foundation Models and Classical Forecasters

Keeps the registered opening question; shorter than the current title; names calibration and the per-series unit, which are the two parts of the contribution others lack.

Trust or Fall Back? Calibrated Selective Forecasting with Time-Series Foundation Models and Classical Methods

Frames the work as selective prediction, matching the risk–coverage evaluation. Less explicit about the per-series unit.

Knowing When Not to Use a Foundation Model: Per-Series Reliability Estimation for Time-Series Forecasting, Including Intermittent Demand

Highlights the failure cases and RQ4. Risks reading as a narrower intermittent-demand study.

Current (registered)
When to Trust a Time-Series Foundation Model: A Per-Series Reliability Framework for Routing Between Foundation Models and Classical Forecasting Models

Accurate but long and repetitive; does not mention calibration.

13Questions for discussion

  1. Given TimeRouter, TW3Cast, Zhang et al., CARFS and FAME, is calibrated reliability plus an explicit defer policy a sufficiently distinct and appropriately scoped contribution?
  2. Corpus: approve a three-role design — GIFT-Eval for broad comparability; M5, Favorita, Car Parts and possibly NN5 for sparse-demand comparison; Ausgrid as a held-out energy field?
  3. Pool: is adding global LightGBM as a required baseline, with deep trained models only as optional comparators, the right scope?
  4. Validation: should the main generalisation claim require leave-one-dataset-out testing plus the held-out Ausgrid field, rather than a random split across series?
  5. Leakage: is a temporal hold-out acceptable where pre-training overlap cannot be ruled out, or should every uncertain model–dataset pair be excluded from headline results?
  6. Licences: are non-commercial weights (MOIRAI), Kaggle-rule data (M5), and the verified upstream terms for Favorita, NN5, Ausgrid and Alibaba acceptable for the artefact?
  7. Title: is a change preferred now, or after M1, given the ethics record?
